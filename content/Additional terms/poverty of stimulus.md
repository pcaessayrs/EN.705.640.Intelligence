_The low proportion of information relative to the expected amount of information needed to account for all rules of a system._

In general, a [[learning]] system trains to become more correct in problem-solving by exposure to [[information]]. Noam Chomsky argues that humans learn [[language]] despite being exposed to a small fraction of its semantic rules, which he dubs as a "poverty of stimulus" in language learning. 

It can be argued that [[👶 The newborn brain|newborns]] receive relatively little direct instruction on the various subjects that they proceed to learn (e.g. language, as above) and instead [[Intelligence is generalizable|generalize]] efficiently from the inputs of their sensory organs (e.g. hearing words spoken around or to them). However, others may argue that being a human is actually an extremely information-dense sensory experience, and this may be [[@markramIntenseWorldTheory2010|subjectively different from person to person]].

Poverty of stimulus may be contrasted against "overfitting," the tendency for a [[artificial neural network]] to fail at generalization by becoming too aligned on its immediate inputs.
